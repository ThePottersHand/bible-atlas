    Threads

    Journeys laid on the land like thread. Choose one and the cord unspools, stop by stop, with the camera following. The Scarlet Thread follows the line of promise from Eden to the ends of the earth.

    Layers

    Boundaries are painted soft-edged on purpose. Scripture gives borders as lists of towns and landmarks, not surveyed lines, and every polygon here is an approximation. Places outside the chosen era are shown faded. Relief is exaggerated three times by default, as on a museum model; “Stand here” drops it to true scale.

    The Land Bears Witness

    A relief atlas of the Bible. Real elevation data is sculpted into a model of the biblical world, from Rome to Ur and from Egypt to Ararat, and lit differently for each era of the story. Nothing modern is drawn: no roads, no borders, no present-day cities. Only the land, its waters, and what Scripture names.

    How to use it

    • Drag to pan, right-drag to tilt and turn, scroll to zoom toward the cursor, double-click to dive in.
    • Click a place to raise its stele: Scripture, history, how sure we are of the site, and what it points to.
    • The Scroll along the bottom moves you through the eras. The light on the land changes with each one, and so do the boundaries and the places that matter.
    • Threads lays journeys on the land. Press play and the cord unspools while the camera follows. Click the numbered stops to jump.
    • Stand here drops you to eye level at a site at true scale. Drag to look around. Moses on Nebo, Elijah on Carmel, Jesus on the Mount of Olives.
    • Layers chooses which boundaries to paint, turns on prophecy pins and rivers, and sets relief exaggeration.
    • The measuring cord gives the distance between any two points and how many days it would take on foot.

    Notes on the map: how we handled uncertainty

    • Site confidence. Every place carries a grade: certain, probable, traditional, disputed or unknown. Where scholars disagree (Mount Sinai, Ai, Sodom, Emmaus, the Red Sea crossing, Bethany beyond the Jordan, Kadesh-barnea) the stele lists the other proposed locations and lets you fly to them. A small ? on a label marks a disputed or unknown site.
    • Boundaries are approximate by nature and drawn soft-edged. Empires in particular are zones of control, not surveyed borders; the desert edges are arbitrary.
    • Dates follow the traditional (early) chronology: the Exodus in 1446 BC after 1 Kings 6:1, the conquest from about 1406 BC, and Jesus’ crucifixion in AD 33 (AD 30 is also widely held). Where the thirteenth-century Exodus date affects a site, the notes say so.
    • Water. The Dead Sea is drawn at about its biblical level (roughly 395 m below sea level), so its southern basin is water, as it was in antiquity and is not today. The Sea of Galilee stands at 205 m below. Modern reservoirs are not drawn. Coastlines are modern; changes since antiquity (the silting of Ephesus and Miletus, Tyre’s causeway) are noted on the relevant steles.
    • Rivers are sketched by hand at coarse resolution. The Nile’s extinct Pelusiac branch is drawn because it mattered for Goshen and the route to Sinai.
    • Relief is exaggerated three times by default so that the land reads like a museum model. “Stand here” and the Layers slider give true scale.
    • Journey routes between named stops are reconstructions along plausible roads and sea lanes, not revealed itineraries.
